Labour MPs Voice Alarm as Milburn Review Set to Overhaul Benefits for Under-25s

By LUCAS HARRINGTON

November 16, 2026

Andy Burnham’s government is bracing for a fresh Labour row over welfare ahead of Tuesday’s publication of Alan Milburn’s long-awaited review of young people who are not in education, employment or training, known as Neets. The former Labour health secretary is expected to recommend that under-25s should no longer be able to claim the health element of Universal Credit or Personal Independence Payment (PIP), according to a report in The Times that has since been followed up by the Press Association, LBC and other outlets.

Under the plans as reported, those young people would move instead to a new youth allowance paid at the same level as the standard rate of Universal Credit. Milburn has acknowledged that the changes will not be painless and that some claimants could receive less financial support than they do now, a prospect that has already unsettled Labour MPs, charities and disability campaigners before the report has even been published.

What the Review Is Expected to Propose

The review, which follows an interim report published in May, is expected to take people under 25 out of the main sickness and disability benefits and replace them with the flat youth allowance. Personnel Today reported that some young people could also qualify for a new additional cost allowance to help with the day-to-day costs of a health condition or disability, while those with more severe disabilities who cannot work would be eligible for a separate benefit paid at a higher rate.

Around four million people claim PIP in England and Wales, about 430,000 of them aged 16 to 24, according to Personnel Today. Milburn has acknowledged that the approach would need substantial upfront investment and could take ten years to deliver savings. He is expected to recommend billions of pounds more for employment support, likely to include work placements, wage subsidies and personal advisers, and young people who do not take up the support on offer could risk losing some of their benefits.

Milburn has also called for a legal “opportunity guarantee”, a duty on government to ensure every young person has access to a job, training or employment support, similar to the legal responsibilities councils have towards homeless people. He says the state spends 25 times as much keeping young people on benefits as it does helping them into work, and wants that ratio cut to two to one, LBC reported.

A Growing Problem for Young People and the Public Purse

The number of Neets topped one million earlier this year for the first time in more than a decade. Milburn has previously warned that the figure could reach one in six young people, or 1.25 million, by 2031, and has estimated that the problem costs the UK around £125 billion a year once lost taxes and higher health and welfare spending are counted, the Press Association reported. Figures published earlier this month showed that 45.3% of Neets were classed as disabled in the year to March, a share that has more than doubled in just over a decade. The i newspaper reported that Burnham is also under pressure to rein in a welfare bill of around £330 billion a year, roughly 10% of GDP, having promised to avoid crude cuts.

Labour MPs Divided

The proposals have reopened wounds from last year, when a backbench rebellion forced Keir Starmer to shelve plans to tighten PIP eligibility in order to pass his welfare legislation. Bell Ribeiro-Addy, who took part in that revolt, told BBC Radio 4’s Today programme that she was not sure she could vote for cuts unless it was clear what MPs were approving. She argued that PIP is not an out-of-work benefit and that the cuts seemed to land on disabled young people, many of whom are already in college or work.

Neil Duncan-Jordan and Rachael Maskell, who both temporarily lost the Labour whip after voting against last year’s bill, said there was not yet enough public detail to judge whether they could back the changes. Duncan-Jordan questioned whether Department for Work and Pensions staff would be able to deliver the employment programme.

Not every former rebel is hostile. Dame Meg Hillier defended Milburn’s approach as radical and bold, and very different from what was proposed a year and a half ago, while Dr Simon Opher welcomed the focus on young people’s mental health but said cutting benefits should be a last resort. One Labour minister told the i newspaper that the party was in a better place than last time, with more goodwill towards Burnham, although the detail would be crucial.

Downing Street and Burnham Respond

Downing Street has declined to rule out changes affecting existing claimants, although it insists a safety net will remain for people who cannot work. A No 10 spokeswoman said the government would respond once the reports are published, and that the country can either invest earlier in young people’s success or keep paying the cost of failure through the benefits system, the Press Association reported.

Speaking in Dublin on Friday, Burnham called the review a watershed moment but declined to give a blanket assurance that he would accept every recommendation, LBC reported. Writing in the Guardian over the weekend, he pledged generational reform in which benefits become the backstop rather than the starting point, said young people should be the priority for investment rather than the target for cuts, and promised a response that would not follow the traditional Department for Work and Pensions model. Lord Blunkett, a mentor to Burnham, said any savings from welfare cuts should be redeployed into employment support, while shadow work and pensions secretary Helen Whately said a legal guarantee did not make sense but that moving from cash payments towards employment support was the right direction.

Charities Urge Caution

Charities have urged ministers to proceed carefully. The National Autistic Society said the plan would “live or die” on sequencing, with jobs and tailored support in place before any money is taken away. Almost 269,000 PIP claimants were classed as having autistic spectrum disorders as of 31 July 2026, according to Department for Work and Pensions data analysed by the Press Association. Scope called the proposals concerning, noting that PIP helps with extra costs whether or not someone works, and the Joseph Rowntree Foundation said young disabled people should not have to trade financial security for better employment support.

What Happens Next

Milburn’s review is due on Tuesday 17 November, and the government says it will consider its recommendations in full. Burnham said on Friday that ministers would set out their response next week, though LBC reported that a response is expected in the coming weeks. Personnel Today noted that the precise financial impact on claimants is unlikely to be clear until legislation is published. The central questions remain open: whether existing claimants will be protected, how quickly employment support can be built, and whether Burnham can keep his MPs onside on an issue that has already divided the party.
